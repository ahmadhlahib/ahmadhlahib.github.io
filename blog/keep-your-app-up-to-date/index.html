Technology changes every year: how to keep your app up to date and reliable

Your app works today. In a year, Android and iOS will have new versions, Google and Apple will have new rules, and the libraries inside your app will have security fixes. Apps don't break because of one big event; they age a little every month. Here is how to keep yours current without paying for a rewrite every three years.

By Ahmad El Lahib · freelance developer in Baabda, Lebanon

Why a working app gets old

  • Store rules change every year. Google Play requires apps to target a recent Android version; apps that fall behind are hidden from new users and then can't be updated until they catch up. Apple regularly requires apps to be built with its latest tools.
  • New phones and new system versions. New screen sizes, new permission rules, new battery limits. Something that worked on last year's phone can crash on this year's.
  • Libraries get security fixes. Every app is built on dozens of open-source libraries. When one has a security hole, the fix only reaches your users if someone updates the app.
  • Services you depend on change. Maps, login with Google or Apple, push notifications and other third-party APIs retire old versions on a schedule.

"Latest" is not the goal. "Current and supported" is.

Chasing every new framework is a waste of your money. Each year there are new tools, and most of them don't change anything for your customers. What you need is an app that stays on supported versions, gets security fixes, passes store rules and is fast on the phones your customers use. A new technology is worth adopting when it solves a real problem: lower cost, faster screens, a feature your users ask for.

Eight things that keep an app current

  1. Start on mainstream technology. For Android that means Kotlin and Jetpack Compose; for Android and iPhone together, Kotlin Multiplatform, Swift or Flutter; for the web, well-known frameworks. Tools backed by Google, Apple or Microsoft and used by millions of developers get updates for years. A niche framework can be abandoned.
  2. Own the code and keep it readable. The code should be in a repository in your name, with a short README on how to build and release it. Then any good developer can continue, not only the one who wrote it.
  3. Update in small steps, often. Updating libraries every month or every quarter is a small job. Skipping three years turns it into a rewrite.
  4. Automated tests on the important parts. Tests are what let a developer update libraries with confidence. Without them, every update is a gamble.
  5. Crash reports and monitoring. Tools like Firebase Crashlytics tell you which screen crashed, on which phone, before customers start complaining.
  6. Release gradually. Google Play can send an update to a small share of users first. If something is wrong, you stop the rollout before most people see it.
  7. A written maintenance plan. What is included (system updates, store rules, security fixes, small bugs), how fast problems are answered, and the monthly or yearly price. No surprises.
  8. A yearly technology review. One meeting a year: what changed, what is worth adding (for example an AI feature that saves your customers time), what can wait. You decide with facts, not with fear of missing out.

Where AI fits

Signs your app is falling behind

  • Warnings in your Google Play Console or emails from Apple about deadlines.
  • Crashes or layout problems on new phones.
  • Login, maps or notifications that stop working one day.
  • Screens that feel slow compared with other apps.
  • Your developer says that any change needs "rewriting everything".

FAQ

My app works fine. Do I really need maintenance?

Yes, even if nothing is broken. Google Play requires apps to target a recent Android version, so an app nobody updates is eventually hidden from new users. Security fixes in libraries also only reach your users when the app is updated.

Will I need to rebuild my app every few years?

Not if it is built on mainstream technology and updated in small steps. A full rebuild is usually the bill for years without maintenance, not a normal cost of owning an app.
